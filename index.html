Welcome! I am a second-year PhD student in the Department of Economics at the University of Oklahoma, where I am advised by Professor Margaret Calloway. My primary research interests are development economics and the economics of environment and climate. I study how climate variability and environmental stress shape household welfare, debt, migration, and institutional adaptation in South and Southeast Asia.

Before joining OU, I was a research associate at the International Centre for Integrated Mountain Development (ICIMOD) in Kathmandu, Nepal, contributing to projects on glacier retreat and downstream agricultural vulnerability.

Working Papers
Monsoon Shocks and Household Debt: Evidence from Rural Nepal JMP Working Paper
Laxman Subedi
Under Review, Journal of Development Economics
Paper Slides
This paper studies how monsoon rainfall shocks affect household debt in rural Nepal. Using a panel of 1,200 farm households matched to high-resolution gridded precipitation data from 2010–2020, I exploit year-to-year rainfall variation within districts to identify the causal effect of negative monsoon shocks on informal and formal borrowing. I find that a one-standard-deviation below-average monsoon season increases the probability of taking an informal loan by 14 percentage points and raises outstanding debt by 22% within the same agricultural year. Effects are concentrated among households with little savings and no access to formal credit. The results suggest that incomplete credit markets amplify climate risk exposure at the household level.
Temperature, Agricultural Wages, and Migration Decisions in the Indo-Gangetic Plain Working Paper
with Priya Menon (University of Oklahoma)
Draft available upon request
We examine how rising temperatures affect agricultural day wages and seasonal migration decisions across the Indo-Gangetic Plain. Combining MODIS land surface temperature data with district-level wage records and the NSSO migration module, we find that a 1°C increase in the summer growing-season temperature reduces agricultural wages by 3.2% and raises the probability of short-term out-migration by 2.5 percentage points. Heterogeneity analysis reveals that effects are strongest in districts with lower groundwater availability, pointing to an interaction between heat stress and irrigation capacity.
Did Community Forestry Reduce Deforestation? A Regression Discontinuity Approach in Nepal's Terai Region Working Paper
Laxman Subedi
Presented at AERE Summer Conference 2024
Paper Slides AERE Presentation
I evaluate the deforestation impact of Nepal's 1993 community forestry devolution, which transferred management rights over forest parcels to locally organized user groups. Exploiting sharp GPS-defined administrative boundaries that determined program eligibility, I implement a spatial regression discontinuity design using 30 years of Landsat-derived forest cover data. I find that community forestry reduced annual deforestation rates by 1.8 percentage points relative to comparable government-managed parcels, an effect that strengthens over time as local institutions consolidate. The results are robust to a range of bandwidth selections and placebo boundary tests.
